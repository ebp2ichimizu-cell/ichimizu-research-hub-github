# Can Threat Appeals Sustain Double-Locking Behavior?
## Separating initial behavior from maintaining that behavior over 28 days

**Original study: Takahito Shimada & Takashi Arai (2017)**

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent commentary prepared by the Research Hub to help police practitioners, crime prevention professionals, and other readers understand the study, its methodology, findings, limitations, and practical implications. It is not an explanation provided or endorsed by the original authors. Please consult the original publication for the complete methodology, results, and authors’ interpretations.

---

## What did the study do?

This field experiment examined whether different threat-appeal messages could encourage bicycle users not only to adopt an additional lock, but also to **continue using it over time**.

The final analytic sample included 256 users of four public bicycle parking facilities.

Participants received one of three types of threat information:

- an identifiable-victim story;
- statistical information about bicycle theft; or
- control information.

They also received either high- or low-response-efficacy information about prevention.

Participants’ vulnerability to theft was classified using the security level of the lock already fitted to their bicycle.

Researchers then repeatedly observed whether participants actually used the additional cable lock through 28 days after the intervention.

The findings were complex. More vulnerable participants were more likely to use the additional lock immediately after the intervention, but use declined over time. In particular, the combination of an identifiable-victim message and high-efficacy information did not sustain behavior among highly vulnerable participants as expected.

The study is therefore useful because it distinguishes **getting someone to act once** from **getting the preventive behavior to persist**.

---

## Why was the study conducted?

Crime-prevention communication often uses threat appeals:

- showing a victim story;
- presenting crime statistics; or
- explaining the effectiveness of a preventive measure.

Protection-motivation approaches suggest that people are more likely to act when they perceive both a meaningful threat and an effective response.

However, many preventive behaviors are not one-off actions.

Double-locking a bicycle must be repeated every time the bicycle is parked.

The practical pathway is therefore:

```text
Perceive the threat
      ↓
Believe the response is effective
      ↓
Intend to act
      ↓
Act once
      ↓
Repeat the behavior
      ↓
Maintain it as routine
```

An intervention can succeed at one stage and fail at another.

---

## How was the field experiment conducted?

The study took place at four subscription bicycle parking facilities attached to railway stations in a metropolitan city.

Of 287 recruited participants, 256 were included in the analysis after excluding cases in which the additional lock could not be installed or the bicycle could not subsequently be located.

Participants were given a cylinder-type cable lock and randomly assigned to different message conditions.

### Three threat-information conditions

**Identifiable-victim condition**  
A narrative describing a specific bicycle-theft victim.

**Statistical condition**  
Information about the prevalence and frequency of bicycle theft.

**Control condition**  
Information unrelated to the theft threat.

### Two response-efficacy conditions

**High-efficacy condition**  
Information emphasizing the effectiveness of double-locking.

**Low-efficacy condition**  
Information emphasizing other preventive measures, making the effectiveness of double-locking less salient.

Vulnerability was additionally classified from the type of existing lock on the bicycle.

---

## What was measured?

The study did not rely only on self-reported intentions.

Researchers returned to the bicycle parking facilities and repeatedly observed whether the additional lock was actually being used, from the day after the intervention through day 28.

The study therefore separated:

```text
Read the message
      ↓
Intend to double-lock
      ↓
Use the second lock immediately
      ↓
Continue using it
      ↓
Maintain the behavior weeks later
```

---

## What did the study find?

### 1. Behavioral intention predicted immediate behavior

Participants with stronger intentions to double-lock were more likely to use the additional lock the next day.

However, intention did not adequately explain the subsequent rate of decline.

In other words, **intending to act helped predict the first action, but did not guarantee maintenance**.

### 2. More vulnerable participants were more responsive initially

Participants whose existing locks made them more vulnerable to theft showed higher immediate use of the additional lock.

Perceived or objective vulnerability may therefore be relevant to initiating preventive action.

### 3. Message type affected how behavior decayed

Use of the additional lock declined over time.

The identifiable-victim condition showed a steeper decline than the control condition.

Among highly vulnerable participants, the combination of identifiable-victim information and high-response-efficacy information did not sustain behavior as expected.

Statistical information did not show the same clear pattern of accelerated decline relative to the control condition.

### 4. Friction in repeated behavior became visible

In the follow-up survey, reasons for not using the additional lock included:

- being in a hurry or forgetting: 47.4%;
- inconvenience: 23.7%;
- difficulty using the lock: 22.7%; and
- believing double-locking was unnecessary for safety: 8.8%.

These results point to a practical bottleneck: **the repeated effort required to perform the behavior.**

## Check the statistics

The key statistical distinction is between initiating double-locking and maintaining it over 28 days.

### What was compared?

The analytic sample contained **256 participants**.

Participants were randomly exposed to combinations of:

- threat information: identifiable victim / statistical victim / control; and
- response efficacy: high / low.

They were also classified as **high or low vulnerability** according to the security level of their existing bicycle lock.

Actual use of the additional lock was observed **five times over 28 days**.

### Immediate behavior

Among 209 participants whose bicycles were observed the next day, double-locking rates across experimental conditions ranged from:

**22.7% to 66.7%.**

Vulnerability was significantly associated with next-day double-locking:

**χ²(1) = 7.29, p < .01**

By contrast, the simple associations for threat-message type and response efficacy were not statistically clear:

- threat type: **χ²(2) = 0.29, ns**
- response efficacy: **χ²(1) = 2.43, ns**

### Behavioral intention: initiation versus maintenance

The mixed-effects generalized linear model separated the initial level of behavior from its subsequent decay.

Behavioral intention predicted next-day double-locking:

**IRR = 1.43, 95% CI [1.14, 1.80], p < .001**

But its effect on the rate of behavioral decay was not statistically clear:

**IRR = 1.17, 95% CI [0.90, 1.53]**

### How should IRR be read?

An IRR above 1 indicates a positive association and an IRR below 1 a negative association.

If the 95% confidence interval includes 1, the data remain compatible with no clear difference.

The result therefore supports a practical distinction:

**intention helped predict initial action, but did not clearly prevent later decline.**

### Did the identifiable-victim message work the same way for everyone?

No.

Under the high-response-efficacy condition, the effect of identifiable-victim information on behavioral maintenance was:

**IRR = 0.29, 95% CI [0.09, 0.92], p = .04**

Among high-vulnerability participants, the identifiable-victim × response-efficacy interaction was:

**IRR = 0.06, 95% CI [0.01, 0.74], p = .03**

Within the high-efficacy / high-vulnerability subgroup, the identifiable-victim effect was:

**IRR = 0.10, 95% CI [0.01, 0.96], p = .05**

These values indicate particularly steep behavioral decay under that combination.

### What did participants say stopped them?

Among 76 participants who reported not using the extra lock in at least one relevant situation:

- **47.4%** said they were in a hurry;
- **23.7%** said double-locking was inconvenient;
- **22.7%** said the lock was difficult to use; and
- **8.8%** believed it was safe without double-locking.

### What did the statistics help decompose?

Instead of summarizing the intervention as:

> threat messaging did not sustain locking,

the analysis shows:

```text
Intention
   ↓
predicted immediate action
   ↓
did not clearly prevent later decay
   ↓
message effects depended on vulnerability and efficacy framing
   ↓
behavioral friction was also present
```

This generates two separate next hypotheses:

- tailor communication to recipient vulnerability; and
- reduce the friction of repeated preventive behavior.

## How should the findings be interpreted?

### What the study directly shows

Message exposure, vulnerability, intention, initial behavior, and behavioral maintenance did not move in exactly the same way.

The intervention process therefore contains multiple stages.

### What the study reasonably suggests

The most persuasive or emotionally vivid message may not necessarily be the best message for sustaining a repeated preventive behavior.

Communication strategies may need to differ depending on:

- the vulnerability of the recipient; and
- whether the desired action is one-off or repetitive.

### What the study does not establish

The outcome was double-locking behavior, not bicycle-theft victimization.

The study therefore does not directly establish how much theft was prevented.

Participants also volunteered for the campaign, which may mean they were more prevention-oriented than typical users.

# Research Hub commentary: moving from persuasion to behavioral maintenance

The practical value of the experiment is not simply identifying which threat message was “best.”

It decomposes a prevention pathway:

```text
Understand the risk
     ↓
Believe the response works
     ↓
Form an intention
     ↓
Act initially
     ↓
Repeat the action
     ↓
Maintain it
```

The intervention was able to influence early stages, but behavior still declined.

That changes the practical diagnosis.

Instead of concluding:

> the message failed,

practitioners can ask:

> **where did the behavior begin to break down?**

The follow-up responses suggest concrete sources of friction:

- forgetting;
- being in a hurry;
- inconvenience; and
- difficulty using the lock.

The next intervention can therefore be designed around behavioral maintenance—for example, point-of-action reminders, lower-friction locking devices, or habit-support mechanisms.

Those specific solutions were not tested in this study. Their value here is that the evaluation generates **testable next hypotheses** rather than merely a pass/fail judgment.

---

## What should practitioners measure?

A similar evaluation could separately measure:

1. message exposure;
2. understanding of the threat;
3. perceived effectiveness of the response;
4. intention;
5. first use of the preventive behavior;
6. one-week maintenance;
7. one-month maintenance; and
8. reasons for discontinuation.

This makes it possible to identify the stage at which the intervention pathway breaks down.

---

## Related theories

### Protection Motivation Theory

Preventive action depends not only on perceived threat but also on beliefs about whether the recommended response is effective and feasible.

### Threat appeal

Threat appeals use information about harm or risk to motivate preventive behavior.

This study is particularly useful because it compares **individual victim narratives with statistical information** and follows actual behavior over time.

### Habit and behavioral cost

Repeated preventive actions depend on more than persuasion. Effort, convenience, and habit formation become increasingly important as time passes.

---

## Key takeaway

**Getting someone to act once and getting them to keep acting are different problems.**

This study showed that:

- vulnerability was related to initial action;
- intention predicted next-day behavior;
- behavior declined over time;
- the pattern of decline differed by message type; and
- practical friction became visible in follow-up responses.

EBP allows practitioners to move beyond saying that “awareness is low.”

Instead, the question becomes:

**At which stage does preventive behavior stop, and what should be changed next?**

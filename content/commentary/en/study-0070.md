# Testing Shoplifting-Prevention Cues Through a Simulated Shoplifting Experiment
## Separating awareness, hesitation, emotional response, and heart-rate response

**Representative source: Okubo et al. (2026), “Examining Effective Shoplifting Prevention Measures in Retail Stores”**

**Related records:**
- study-0033: Kagawa University’s 2025 implementation and preliminary release
- study-0071: Ohyama et al. (2026), physiological-response analysis from the same experiment

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent commentary prepared by the Research Hub to help police practitioners, crime prevention professionals, and other readers understand the study, its methodology, findings, limitations, and practical implications. It is not an explanation provided or endorsed by the original authors. Please consult the original publication for the complete methodology, results, and authors’ interpretations.

## What did this research series do?

The experiment placed university and postgraduate students in a real retail environment and asked them to perform a **simulated shoplifting task** while being exposed to six different prevention cues:

- a flashing light;
- an anxiety-arousing sound;
- a calming sound;
- a sign stating that the CCTV camera was being adjusted;
- an image of watching eyes; and
- a greeting from a member of staff.

There were **46 participants**.

Two 2026 conference abstracts analyse the same experiment from different outcome perspectives.

study-0070 focuses on:

- whether participants noticed the cue;
- whether they reported hesitating before the simulated act; and
- changes in mood measured with the short POMS scale.

study-0071 focuses on:

- heart-rate responses.

The key value of the series is therefore not merely to rank “which cue worked best”, but to separate several stages of response:

**notice → hesitate → emotional reaction → physiological reaction.**

The experiment did not involve actual shoplifters and did not measure changes in real shoplifting incidents.

---

## Why was the experiment conducted?

Retail crime prevention offers many practical options—CCTV, signs, sound, lighting, staff engagement and other environmental cues.

However, popularity or visibility does not demonstrate effectiveness.

Testing these measures with real offending is ethically and operationally difficult.

The researchers therefore used a simulated task in a real store, allowing several prevention cues to be compared under relatively controlled conditions while retaining some ecological realism.

Kagawa University reports that the experiment was supported by Kagawa Prefectural Police, IC Co., Ltd. and Tokio Marine & Nichido Fire Insurance Co., Ltd., within the longer Kagawa University–Kagawa Police shoplifting-prevention collaboration.

---

## What happened in the experiment?

The study was conducted in October 2025 at a home-improvement store in Kagawa Prefecture.

Plain-clothes security staff identified six locations considered vulnerable to shoplifting.

Participants repeatedly carried a researcher-prepared item to the target location and placed it in a bag while trying not to be observed.

Each participant experienced all six prevention conditions.

The companion study, study-0071, reports that participants were allocated to six different starting-order groups, with the starting condition randomised to reduce order effects.

The design is therefore a **within-participant repeated-measures field experiment**, not six independent groups.

---

## What did the studies find?

### Awareness and hesitation did not move in the same way

In study-0070, the six conditions differed significantly in whether participants noticed the prevention measure.

The CCTV-adjustment sign was more often unnoticed than other conditions.

The conditions also differed in self-reported hesitation.

The staff-greeting condition produced the highest tendency to report hesitation, and the flashing-light and two sound conditions produced more hesitation than the CCTV-adjustment sign.

### Staff greetings changed several mood indicators

The POMS analysis showed differences in tension/anxiety, fatigue/inertia, confusion/bewilderment and depression/dejection.

The staff-greeting condition was higher than baseline on all four of these indicators.

The flashing-light and anxiety-sound conditions also increased fatigue/inertia relative to baseline.

These measures show psychological response.

They do not themselves demonstrate crime prevention.

### Heart rate produced a somewhat different pattern

The companion analysis in study-0071 compared post-cue heart rate while adjusting for pre-cue heart rate and other factors.

Compared with the flashing-light condition, higher heart rates were observed under:

- anxiety sound;
- calming sound;
- the CCTV-adjustment sign; and
- staff greeting.

The sound effects were weaker when the cue occurred more than five seconds before the simulated act.

A particularly useful lesson is that the CCTV-adjustment sign showed relatively weak conscious awareness and hesitation in study-0070 but a higher heart-rate response than the flashing-light condition in study-0071.

**Physiological arousal and consciously reported deterrence are not the same outcome.**

## Check the statistics

### Participants and design

There were **46 participants**.

All experienced the six conditions, making this a repeated-measures design.

The companion abstract reports randomisation of the starting condition across six order groups.

This helps reduce individual differences, but repeated exposure can still introduce practice, fatigue and carry-over effects.

### Awareness and hesitation

study-0070 used Cochran’s Q tests.

Awareness:

**Q = 59.750, df = 5, p < .001**

Hesitation:

**Q = 82.960, df = 5, p < .001**

These results indicate that the six conditions did not produce the same response proportions.

They do not estimate how much real shoplifting would decline.

### Mood

Significant condition differences were reported for:

- tension–anxiety: **F(6,270)=12.192, p<.001**
- fatigue–inertia: **F(6,270)=6.114, p<.001**
- confusion–bewilderment: **F(6,270)=4.517, p<.001**
- depression–dejection: **F(6,270)=3.083, p<.001**

The staff-greeting condition was higher than baseline on all four measures.

### Heart rate

study-0071 used a linear mixed model.

With flashing light as the reference condition:

- anxiety sound: **β = 8.096, p = .007**
- calming sound: **β = 11.206, p = .003**
- CCTV-adjustment sign: **β = 11.254, p < .001**
- staff greeting: **β = 5.853, p = .036**

The effects of the two sound conditions were weaker when the cue appeared more than five seconds before the act.

### The most important statistical lesson

The outcomes are not interchangeable:

```text
awareness
  ≠ hesitation
  ≠ emotional state
  ≠ heart rate
  ≠ actual offending
  ≠ store-level shoplifting
```

A cue can produce physiological arousal without producing the strongest self-reported hesitation, and vice versa.

### Where to verify

**study-0070**
- six experimental conditions;
- Cochran’s Q tests for awareness and hesitation;
- POMS analyses.

**study-0071**
- repeated-measures allocation;
- continuous heart-rate measurement;
- mixed-effects model;
- timing interactions.

## How should the findings be interpreted?

### Directly supported

Different prevention cues produced different patterns across awareness, hesitation, mood and heart-rate outcomes.

Staff greeting was associated with relatively high self-reported hesitation and changes across several mood measures.

Several conditions also produced higher heart rates than flashing light.

### Reasonably suggested

Retail prevention measures should be evaluated at the level of the **mechanism or response stage** they are intended to influence.

Social interaction, environmental signage, sound and visual cues may operate through different pathways.

### Not established

The studies do not establish that:

- staff greetings cause the largest reduction in real shoplifting;
- higher heart rate means stronger crime prevention;
- signs are ineffective in real-world offenders; or
- the findings generalise to other stores or offender populations.

---

## Limitations

### Participants were not real offenders

Students performed a research task. Their motivation, risk perception and emotional state may differ substantially from those of actual offenders.

### There was no independent no-intervention shoplifting condition

The study compared six active cues. It did not directly estimate the absolute effect of each cue relative to an identical simulated act with no prevention measure.

### Repeated exposure

Each person completed six simulated acts. Although order was addressed, learning and habituation may remain.

### Single retail setting

Generalisability across store layouts, staff models and customer populations is unknown.

# Research Hub commentary: measure where the intervention acts

The strongest EBP lesson is not “use greeting rather than posters”.

It is that an intervention can act at different stages:

```text
cue is present
      ↓
person notices it
      ↓
person hesitates
      ↓
emotional/physiological response
      ↓
person abandons the act
      ↓
store-level theft declines
```

The current research mainly measures the middle stages.

The final two stages—actual abandonment of offending and reduction in shoplifting—remain to be tested.

This makes the experiment useful as a **candidate-screening stage**, not as the final outcome evaluation.

---

## What should the next evaluation measure?

Promising conditions could be taken forward to multi-store evaluation measuring:

- actual loss or incident outcomes;
- durability over time and habituation;
- customer or staff side effects;
- implementation fidelity;
- staffing and equipment cost;
- the timing and manner of staff greetings.

A useful sequence would be:

**simulation to identify candidates → multi-store implementation → crime/loss outcomes → cost and side-effect assessment.**

---

## Related concepts

### Situational crime prevention

The measures attempt to alter perceived risk, attention, effort or psychological cost at the point of opportunity.

### Implementation feasibility

Staff greetings require labour, training and consistency. Environmental cues may be easier to deploy but may be unnoticed, habituated to or produce unintended customer effects.

Effectiveness and implementability therefore need to be considered separately.

---

## Key takeaway

**Do not choose a shoplifting-prevention measure from a single outcome.**

In this experiment:

- staff greeting produced relatively high self-reported hesitation;
- several mood indicators also changed;
- heart-rate patterns were different again; and
- the CCTV-adjustment sign illustrates that physiological response and conscious hesitation can diverge.

The next EBP step is to test whether these intermediate responses translate into actual reductions in shoplifting.

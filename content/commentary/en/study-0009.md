# Effects of a Community Crime-Prevention Program on Perceived Public Safety and Fear of Crime

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent commentary prepared by the Research Hub to help police practitioners and crime-prevention professionals understand the study and its evidential limits. It is not an explanation provided or endorsed by the original authors. Please consult the original source for the exact study details.

The study evaluated Chiba Prefecture's convenience-store crime-prevention box program at two sites.

Its key contribution is conceptual: **perceived neighborhood safety, perceived victimization risk, and fear of crime are not the same outcome.**

Residents generally reported improved neighborhood safety after implementation, yet some residents with greater exposure to the intervention reported higher perceived risk and fear.

<!-- STATS_DETAIL_START -->

## Check the statistics

Valid responses:

- Chiba: 762 pre / 728 post
- Ichikawa: 619 pre / 605 post

Perceived safety improved:

- Chiba “better”: 3.7% → 7.6%; “worse”: 16.0% → 6.2%, p<.001
- Ichikawa “better”: 3.9% → 5.8%; “worse”: 10.7% → 6.6%, p<.05

In Chiba, among residents within a 10-minute walk after implementation:

- perceived-risk scale: 9.71 vs 8.79, p=.009
- fear-of-crime scale: 10.28 vs 9.52, p=.044

These associations do not establish that the program caused fear to rise. Exposure was not randomized and neighborhoods may differ.

## Research Hub commentary

A visible prevention program may simultaneously produce:

**reassurance** and **heightened awareness of crime risk**.

Evaluation should therefore measure multiple psychological outcomes separately.

## Limitations

- not a full panel design;
- non-randomized exposure;
- multi-component intervention;
- local context may affect results.

## Key takeaway

Do not use one “sense of safety” item as a proxy for all resident reactions.

# An Environmental-Cue Nudge Intervention for Street-Based Solicitation for Prostitution
## Can changing the meaning of a place change behavior?

**Research presentation: Shimada, Kitagawa, Nakagawa & Ohtake (2025)**

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent commentary prepared by the Research Hub to help police practitioners, crime prevention professionals, and other readers understand the study, its methodology, findings, limitations, and practical implications. It is not an explanation provided or endorsed by the presenters. Please consult the original source for the exact study description.
>
> **Publication status**  
> The J-STAGE source is a **one-page oral-presentation abstract** from the 18th Annual Meeting of the Japanese Society of Environmental Psychology. The Research Hub does not infer statistical tests or causal effects that are not reported in that source.

---

## What did the study do?

The study addressed street-based solicitation for prostitution concentrated in a narrow alley in a major Osaka entertainment district.

Rather than relying only on enforcement, warning signs, patrol, or public information, the project asked:

> **Could changing the appearance and social meaning of the place change the behavior occurring there?**

From May to December 2024, Sonezaki Police Station worked with local partners on a place-based intervention that included:

1. a street café organized by a local crime-prevention group;
2. pavement art produced through a local design-school internship; and
3. repainting approximately 100 metres of the alley yellow and installing ten pavement artworks, each about one square metre.

---

## Why focus on the place?

Before designing the intervention, the team analyzed police records concerning **28 women who had been arrested**, with a mean age of 24.4 years.

Reported sources through which participants had learned about the location included:

- customers/men: 35.7%;
- friends: 32.1%; and
- the internet: 28.6%.

Reasons for choosing the location included:

- a larger share of earnings: 14.3%; and
- encouragement from others: 10.7%.

This suggests that the alley was not simply a physically convenient location. It may also have acquired a shared social meaning as a place where solicitation occurred.

---

## The problem-oriented policing sequence

The abstract explicitly describes a four-stage problem-solving process:

**identify → analyze → respond → assess**

This parallels the SARA model:

- Scanning
- Analysis
- Response
- Assessment

The intervention can therefore be represented as:

```text
solicitation concentrated in a specific alley
      ↓
analyze why the place is selected
      ↓
change environmental and social cues
      ↓
observe behavior before and after
```

---

## What exactly was changed?

The most visible change was repainting about 100 metres of the alley **yellow** and adding ten aquarium-themed pavement artworks.

But the project should not be interpreted as a clean test of yellow pavement alone.

During the broader initiative there were also:

- a street café;
- design-school participation;
- neighborhood involvement;
- municipal work on the street surface; and
- ongoing police activity.

It is therefore better understood as a **multi-component place intervention intended to change environmental cues and the meaning of the space**.

---

## How was the outcome measured?

Police officers from headquarters and the local station observed both the intervention alley and an adjacent alley where displacement might occur.

Observation periods were:

- two weeks before construction: 11–17 November;
- six weeks after completion: 3–9 February;
- eight weeks after completion: 17–23 February.

On each of the seven days, observations were made four times:

- 14:00
- 17:00
- 20:00
- 22:00

That produced **28 observation points per period**.

---

## What did the study find?

The abstract reports an approximately **90% reduction** in the number of people waiting for customers after the intervention.

The mean number observed per time point was:

| Period | Intervention alley | Adjacent displacement alley |
|---|---:|---:|
| Before intervention | 7.43 | 0.00 |
| 6 weeks after | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| 8 weeks after | 0.86 | 0.13 |

The presenters concluded that no marked displacement was observed in the adjacent alley.

## Check the statistics


### What numbers are available?

Each period contained:

**7 days × 4 observations per day = 28 observation points.**

The publicly available abstract reports only descriptive averages:

| Period | Intervention alley | Adjacent alley |
|---|---:|---:|
| Pre-intervention | 7.43 | 0.00 |
| 6 weeks post | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| 8 weeks post | 0.86 | 0.13 |

The abstract summarizes this as an approximately **90% decline** in solicitation waiting.

---

### Are p-values or confidence intervals reported?

No.

The one-page conference abstract does not report:

- a test statistic;
- p-value;
- confidence interval; or
- effect size.

The Research Hub therefore does **not** describe the reduction as “statistically significant.”

What can be stated is that a large descriptive reduction was observed and remained evident at the six- and eight-week observations.

---

### Why does the absence of a control location matter?

There was no untreated comparison location observed under the same design.

The adjacent alley was used to look for **spatial displacement**, not as a standard control group.

The presenters themselves state that future evaluation should include a control location to address **seasonality**.

Other changes that may have differed between November and February include:

- weather and temperature;
- pedestrian flow;
- police activity;
- other social conditions; and
- the broader mix of interventions.

These factors cannot be fully separated from the environmental intervention in this design.

---

### What about displacement?

The adjacent alley averages were:

- 0.00 before;
- 0.00 at six weeks;
- 0.13 at eight weeks.

This does not show a large shift into the immediately adjacent alley.

But the study did not systematically measure:

- more distant areas;
- other entertainment districts;
- changes in time of day; or
- movement to other forms of solicitation.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore:

> **no marked displacement was observed in the adjacent alley that was monitored.**

---

### What does the analysis of 28 people tell us?

Among the 28 people whose police records were analyzed:

- 78.6% had experience in the sex industry;
- 82.1% had experience in other employment;
- 64.3% identified debt as a motivation;
- 64.3% identified payments to host clubs as a motivation.

These figures show that place-based behavior was embedded in broader economic and social circumstances.

The environment was therefore only one part of the behavioral system.

---

### What did the numbers help decompose?

A simplistic summary is:

> the road was painted yellow and solicitation fell by 90%.

A more precise sequence is:

```text
behavior was concentrated at one place
      ↓
records were analyzed to understand why the place was selected
      ↓
environmental and social cues were changed
      ↓
the observed number in the intervention alley fell sharply
      ↓
no large increase was observed in the adjacent alley
      ↓
but seasonality and other concurrent changes were not controlled
```

That is the level of inference supported by the public abstract.

## How should the findings be interpreted?

### What the source directly supports

The project:

- identified a highly concentrated place-based problem;
- analyzed police records for 28 people;
- implemented a substantial environmental intervention;
- repeatedly observed behavior before and after;
- found a large descriptive reduction in the intervention alley; and
- monitored an adjacent alley for possible displacement.

### What the findings reasonably suggest

Environmental cues and the social meaning of a place may influence whether that place continues to be used for a particular behavior.

For police problem-solving, this supports asking not only:

> whom should we enforce against?

but also:

> **why is this place repeatedly selected?**

### What the study cannot establish by itself

It cannot determine:

- the effect of yellow paint alone;
- the effect of pavement art alone;
- the independent effect of the street café;
- the effect independent of ongoing enforcement;
- a seasonally adjusted causal effect;
- displacement to more distant locations;
- whether people stopped the behavior or changed location;
- effects on health, exploitation, safety, or economic circumstances; or
- long-term persistence.

---

## Limitations and alternative explanations

### 1. No control site
The presenters explicitly identify this as a future evaluation need.

### 2. Multi-component intervention
Several environmental and community activities occurred, so individual components cannot be isolated.

### 3. Short follow-up
The public abstract reports follow-up through eight weeks after construction.

### 4. The outcome is presence in one location
It does not directly measure whether prostitution, exploitation, or underlying vulnerability declined.

---

## Post-presentation information

> **The following is not part of the research analysis. It is later media reporting and should be treated separately.**

A July 2025 Kansai TV report stated that local crime-prevention representatives had begun to see one or two people returning from around April, and that police later arrested five people on suspicion of violating prostitution-related law.

This is not a formal longitudinal evaluation and does not establish that the intervention “stopped working.”

It does, however, indicate that the large reduction documented through eight weeks should not automatically be assumed to persist indefinitely.

---

# Research Hub commentary: the key lesson is not the color yellow

The most useful feature of this study is not a claim that:

> yellow roads reduce solicitation.

The stronger problem-solving lesson is that the team first asked:

> **why is this behavior concentrated in this particular 100-metre alley?**

Information about the location appeared to circulate through people and the internet.

That creates a possible reinforcing process:

```text
the place becomes known for the behavior
      ↓
people are directed there
      ↓
visible activity reinforces the place's meaning
      ↓
more people select the same place
```

The environmental intervention can be understood as an attempt to disrupt that **place meaning**.

---

## Do not stop at making the behavior less visible

A reduction in visible solicitation can be an important local outcome.

But the records also indicate debt, financial pressure, interpersonal influence, and other vulnerabilities.

A stronger future evaluation would therefore combine:

```text
presence in the target place
      +
displacement
      +
re-emergence
      +
connection to support services
      +
safety and living conditions
```

Place-based prevention and support-oriented responses do not have to be treated as alternatives.

---

## What should practitioners measure in a replication?

At minimum:

1. baseline counts;
2. time-of-day patterns;
3. immediate postintervention change;
4. 1-, 3-, and 6-month persistence;
5. displacement to adjacent areas;
6. displacement to more distant areas;
7. police activity;
8. legitimate use of the space by residents and pedestrians;
9. connection to support services; and
10. perceptions of the people directly affected.

Where feasible, an untreated comparison location should be observed at the same time.

---

## Related frameworks

### Problem-Oriented Policing / SARA
The study explicitly follows a problem-solving sequence from problem identification through evaluation.

### Environmental cues
Color, design, activity, and visible use of space can influence what people perceive a location to be “for” and what behavior appears normal there.

---

## Key takeaway

The study does **not** establish that yellow pavement itself caused the outcome.

What it demonstrates is a practical problem-solving sequence:

**analyze why behavior concentrates at a place**
↓
**change the environmental conditions**
↓
**measure behavior afterward**
↓
**check for displacement**
↓
**identify what still needs stronger evaluation**

The next EBP question is not:

> “Should we paint the road yellow?”

It is:

> **“Under what conditions can changing the meaning and cues of a place produce durable behavior change without merely moving the problem elsewhere?”**

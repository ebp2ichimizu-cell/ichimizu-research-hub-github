# Is Recognising Suspicion Enough?
## Breaking a police-impersonation scam into answering, suspicion, continuation and near-disclosure

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is based on Chuo University's official research release of 16 February 2026. A peer-reviewed article has not yet been confirmed. The commentary therefore does not add statistical or psychological claims beyond the official material.

---

## What did the study do?

With cooperation from Kanagawa Prefectural Police, police officers acted as simulated scam callers impersonating police and called Chuo University students on their actual mobile phones.

Participants had previously received information about special fraud and consented to take part in a study involving deception, although the exact purpose, content and timing of the call were withheld.

The study focused on the **process leading towards victimisation**, rather than only a final yes/no outcome.

---

## What happened?

- 148 students consented and were called.
- 25 answered.
- 5 progressed to immediately before disclosing a LINE ID.

The denominator matters:

- 5/25 = **20% of those who answered**;
- 5/148 = **about 3.4% of all consenting participants**.

It would therefore be misleading to say that one in five participants was deceived. No real financial loss or completed disclosure occurred.

The official release also reports that some students continued the conversation even after feeling suspicious.

---

## Check the statistics

- Answer rate: 25/148 ≈ **16.9%**
- Reached near-disclosure among answerers: 5/25 = **20.0%**
- Reached near-disclosure among all participants: 5/148 ≈ **3.4%**

These percentages answer different questions and should not be interchanged.

## Why does the process matter?

A useful sequence is:

incoming call → answer → hear the story → become suspicious → continue or terminate → request for personal information → disclose or refuse.

Crime-prevention interventions can therefore target different stages: reducing unknown-number answering, prompting scam expectations, making it easier to terminate a suspicious call, or encouraging third-party consultation.

The study did **not** test the effectiveness of those interventions.

---

## Limitations

The sample consisted of university students, not the broader population of real victims. Participants had been briefed about fraud and had consented to a deception study. Only 25 answered the call. The work is currently represented by an official research release rather than a confirmed peer-reviewed publication.

---

# Research Hub commentary: measure the gap between knowing and disengaging

Fraud prevention should distinguish knowledge, recognition and action. Knowing about a scam or feeling suspicious does not necessarily mean a person will end the interaction.

Useful intermediate outcomes include answering rates, time to suspicion, termination after suspicion, consultation and refusal of information requests.

---

## Take-away

**Recognising that a call is suspicious and actually disengaging are different behaviours.**

The study is useful for mapping the victimisation process, but it is not an estimate of real-world fraud victimisation rates.

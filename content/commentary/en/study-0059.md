# Promoting Caller-Verification Behavior on Landline Calls to Prevent Special-Fraud Victimization Among Older Adults
## Can an intervention change what people actually do when the phone rings?

**Research presentation: Shimada, Saito, Yamane, Kuraishi, Haruta & Ohtake (2022)**

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent commentary prepared by the Research Hub to help police practitioners, crime prevention professionals, and other readers understand the study, its methodology, findings, limitations, and practical implications. It is not an explanation provided or endorsed by the original authors. Please consult the original conference paper for the exact study details.
>
> **Study-design note**  
> The authors describe the study in their discussion as a field RCT. The methods section states that intervention **days** were assigned to five conditions, but the publicly available conference paper does not describe the randomization procedure in detail. The Research Hub therefore describes it conservatively as a field experiment with condition allocation rather than assuming individual-level randomization.

---

## What did the study do?

The study examined a practical prevention problem:

> **Can an intervention change what older adults actually do when a landline telephone rings, rather than merely changing what they know or intend to do?**

Participants were recruited at two police stations in Shiga Prefecture when visiting for driver's-license renewal or other purposes.

The intervention involved **597 older adults who had a landline telephone at home**.

Five conditions combined:

- a conventional threat message;
- an altruistic message;
- instructions for using an answering-machine function;
- immediate rehearsal with a simulated telephone; and
- a follow-up telephone check the next day.

One week later, police staff called participants' home landlines and observed whether they:

- answered while the phone was still ringing;
- waited until the answering-machine function activated; or
- waited until the caller identified themselves before answering.

The outcome was therefore **observable telephone-answering behavior**, not simply self-reported intention.

---

## Why was the study conducted?

Crime-prevention communication often uses threat appeals:

- describe the fraud technique;
- explain the risk;
- tell people to be careful.

This approach is broadly consistent with Protection Motivation Theory.

The researchers argued, however, that knowledge about fraud techniques may be insufficient.

Landline countermeasures may also be blocked by behavioral factors such as:

- status-quo bias;
- reluctance to send acquaintances to voicemail; and
- procrastination.

The study therefore tested two additional approaches:

1. a **social-norm approach framed through altruistic motivation**; and
2. a **behavior-change approach using behavioral rehearsal**.

---

## What were the five conditions?

### Condition 1: Leaflet A — conventional threat message

Participants were told that fraudsters use persuasive techniques and were shown several countermeasures.

This served as the study's control condition.

### Condition 2: Leaflet B — altruistic message + answering-machine instructions

Participants were told that using an answering machine themselves could encourage friends and others around them to do the same.

The study describes this as a **social-norm approach**.

### Condition 3: Leaflet C — threat message + answering-machine instructions

Participants received threat information and photo-based instructions for using the answering-machine function.

### Condition 4: Leaflet C + immediate rehearsal

After the same instructions as Condition 3, participants practiced on a simulated telephone.

They rehearsed waiting for the answering machine and checking the caller before answering.

### Condition 5: Leaflet C + next-day home call

Police staff called the participant's home the following day to check use of the answering-machine function and provided additional instruction when the participant answered without verifying the caller.

---

## What behavior was measured?

One week after the intervention, police staff called participants' home landlines.

Two main behavioral outcomes were examined.

### 1. Did the participant wait for the answering machine?

This distinguished people who answered during the ringing phase from those who waited until the answering-machine function had activated.

### 2. Did the participant wait until the caller identified themselves?

This represented a more cautious verification behavior.

These are observable actions rather than intentions.

---

## What did the study find?

Of the original 597 participants, **284 participants who personally answered the one-week follow-up call** were included in the behavioral analysis.

### The altruistic message increased caller-verification behavior

Condition 2 produced roughly a **20-percentage-point increase** in answering only after the answering-machine function had activated compared with Condition 1.

### Immediate rehearsal also improved behavior

Condition 4 produced roughly a **20-percentage-point increase** compared with Condition 3, which provided instructions without immediate practice.

### The delayed follow-up condition did not improve behavior

Condition 5 did not produce the same improvement.

The finding therefore distinguishes:

> **being told how to act**

from:

> **actually rehearsing the behavior in the relevant sequence.**

## Check the statistics


### From 597 participants to 284 behavioral observations

The intervention sample consisted of:

- **597 participants**
- 364 men
- 223 women
- mean age **75.90 years**
- SD **6.10**

At the one-week follow-up, **284 participants personally answered the police call** and were included in the behavioral analysis.

This means that the final behavioral analysis covered roughly half of the original intervention sample.

---

### Counts shown in Figure 2

The conference paper's Figure 2 reports the numbers of participants who:

- answered after the caller identified themselves;
- answered after the answering machine activated but before identification; or
- answered while the telephone was still ringing.

| Condition | After caller identification | Before identification | Answered while ringing | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 Threat message | 6 | 11 | 41 | 58 |
| 2 Altruistic message | 7 | 20 | 26 | 53 |
| 3 Instructions | 5 | 9 | 39 | 53 |
| 4 Immediate rehearsal | 13 | 14 | 28 | 55 |
| 5 Next-day check | 4 | 15 | 46 | 65 |
| **Total** | **35** | **69** | **180** | **284** |

---

### Percentage who waited until the answering machine activated

Using the counts in Figure 2, the Research Hub calculates:

| Condition | Answered after answering-machine activation |
|---|---:|
| Condition 1 | 17/58 = **29.3%** |
| Condition 2 | 27/53 = **50.9%** |
| Condition 3 | 14/53 = **26.4%** |
| Condition 4 | 27/55 = **49.1%** |
| Condition 5 | 19/65 = **29.2%** |

These percentages are Research Hub calculations from the published figure counts.

Condition 2 was about **21.6 percentage points** above Condition 1.

Condition 4 was about **22.7 percentage points** above Condition 3.

This matches the authors' description of an approximately 20-point increase.

---

### Was the difference across conditions statistically clear?

For whether participants waited until the answering machine activated:

**χ²(4) = 13.6, p < .01**

The five conditions therefore did not have the same behavioral distribution.

Residual comparisons reported in the paper indicated that **Conditions 2 and 4** had significantly higher rates of answering after answering-machine activation than Conditions 1, 3, and 5 at the 5% level.

---

### What about waiting until the caller identified themselves?

For the stricter outcome—waiting until the caller identified themselves—the overall result was:

**χ²(4) = 9.42, p = .051**

This is just above the conventional .05 threshold and should not be described as a clearly significant overall condition effect.

However, residual analysis indicated that Condition 4 had a higher proportion of participants who waited until identification.

From Figure 2, the Research Hub calculates:

- Condition 1: 10.3%
- Condition 2: 13.2%
- Condition 3: 9.4%
- **Condition 4: 23.6%**
- Condition 5: 6.2%

This suggests that immediate behavioral rehearsal may have influenced the more demanding verification behavior as well.

---

### How cleanly does the altruistic-message comparison isolate the mechanism?

Condition 1 and Condition 2 differ not only in the motivational message but also in the coping information provided.

A cleaner descriptive comparison is:

- Condition 2: altruistic message + answering-machine instructions
- Condition 3: threat message + answering-machine instructions

These two conditions both provided answering-machine instructions.

Their observed rates of waiting for answering-machine activation were:

- Condition 2: **50.9%**
- Condition 3: **26.4%**

The conference paper does not provide a separate pairwise effect size or confidence interval for this contrast, so the difference should not be overinterpreted.

---

### Instructions versus rehearsal

Conditions 3 and 4 used the same Leaflet C.

The key difference was:

- Condition 3: instructions only;
- Condition 4: immediate practice using a simulated telephone.

The observed rates were:

- Condition 3: **26.4%**
- Condition 4: **49.1%**

This is a particularly interpretable comparison for the behavioral-rehearsal component.

---

### What did the statistics help decompose?

A simple summary would be:

> altruistic messaging and rehearsal worked.

The fuller picture is:

```text
597 people received an intervention
      ↓
284 provided observable follow-up behavior
      ↓
behavior differed across five conditions
      ↓
altruistic messaging and immediate rehearsal were about 20 points higher
      ↓
the stricter "wait until identification" outcome was borderline overall (p=.051)
      ↓
even the strongest condition changed behavior in only about half of participants
```

The last point is critical.

**The intervention improved behavior, but it did not solve the problem for everyone.**

## How should the findings be interpreted?

### What the study directly supports

The study:

- implemented interventions at two real police stations;
- involved 597 older adults with home landlines;
- used five intervention conditions;
- measured actual telephone-answering behavior one week later;
- found higher verification behavior in the altruistic-message and immediate-rehearsal conditions;
- did not find similar improvement in the delayed follow-up condition; and
- used an observable behavioral outcome rather than intention alone.

### What the findings reasonably suggest

Fraud prevention may need to move beyond telling people that fraud is dangerous.

It may be useful to:

> **practice the exact behavior that should occur when the phone rings.**

The results also suggest that an altruistic framing—emphasizing how one's behavior can help protect others—may sometimes overcome barriers that self-protection messaging does not.

### What this study cannot establish by itself

It does not establish:

- persistence after one month, six months, or one year;
- reduction in actual fraud victimization;
- effectiveness among older adults who do not visit police stations;
- the exact strength of randomization at the individual level; or
- effects among participants who were not observed at follow-up.

---

## Limitations and alternative explanations

### 1. Behavior was measured at only one follow-up point

The authors identify this as the first major limitation.

The study examined behavior one week later. It does not show whether the behavior became a stable habit.

### 2. Even the strongest condition changed behavior in only about half of participants

The authors explicitly note that preventive behavior remained below complete uptake.

An intervention can therefore show an effect while leaving many people exposed.

### 3. Only 284 of 597 participants entered the behavioral analysis

Participants who did not personally answer the follow-up call were not included in the main behavioral comparison.

The conference paper does not provide enough detail to determine whether follow-up loss differed across conditions.

### 4. Allocation was by intervention day

The methods describe assigning **days** to the five intervention conditions.

Although the authors call the study an RCT in the discussion, the public conference paper does not describe the randomization sequence in detail.

The Research Hub therefore avoids treating it as a clearly documented individual-level RCT.

### 5. The follow-up call was not a real fraud attempt

The observed behavior occurred during a call from police staff.

A real fraud call may involve different:

- emotional pressure;
- conversational tactics;
- timing;
- caller ID; and
- urgency.

Nevertheless, measuring actual telephone behavior is a substantial step beyond measuring intention alone.

# Research Hub commentary: the bottleneck may be execution, not knowledge

The study is valuable because it does not assume that the main problem is simply lack of fraud knowledge.

The prevention pathway can be decomposed as:

```text
know about fraud
      ↓
know that answering-machine screening is useful
      ↓
know how to operate the function
      ↓
when the phone rings, resist answering immediately
      ↓
wait for caller identification
      ↓
repeat the behavior until it becomes routine
```

A public-information campaign may improve the first two stages while failing at the fourth.

The behavioral-rehearsal condition directly targeted that execution gap.

---

## Explaining a behavior is different from rehearsing it

The comparison between Conditions 3 and 4 has a clear operational implication.

Instead of:

```text
show a leaflet
      ↓
explain how the answering machine works
```

a prevention session can include:

```text
ring a simulated telephone
      ↓
do not answer immediately
      ↓
wait for the answering machine
      ↓
listen for caller identification
      ↓
only then respond
```

The study found a substantial difference one week later.

This suggests a possible redesign of crime-prevention education from **information delivery** toward **behavioral practice**.

---

## The next bottleneck is the half who still did not change

Even in the strongest conditions, safe answering behavior was observed in only about half of participants.

The next EBP question is therefore not merely:

> does altruistic messaging work?

It is:

> **why did the remaining participants not change?**

Possible factors to test include:

- difficulty operating the telephone;
- reluctance to miss calls from acquaintances;
- device features;
- strong habitual responding;
- hearing or cognitive limitations;
- household arrangements; and
- optimistic bias about personal victimization.

Those factors should be measured rather than assumed.

---

## What should practitioners measure in a replication?

At minimum:

1. baseline answering habits;
2. availability of answering-machine functions;
3. whether the function is actually enabled;
4. ability to operate the device;
5. immediate comprehension;
6. observed behavior after one week;
7. persistence after one and three months;
8. household influence;
9. exposure to suspicious calls; and
10. fraud attempts, near misses, and victimization.

---

## Related theory and research

### Protection Motivation Theory

Threat appeals used in conventional prevention communication are related to PMT.

This study is useful not because it rejects PMT, but because it shows that:

> **understanding the threat does not guarantee execution of the protective behavior.**

### Behavioral rehearsal

Rehearsal allows people to practice the desired response before the real situation occurs.

### [Experimental Study of Public-Sector Crime-Prevention Communication](#/study/study-0021)
**Experimental study of crime-prevention information dissemination by a public agency (2023)**

That study found changes in some intentions and efficacy beliefs but less clear change in actual protective behavior.

study-0059 can be read as a related next step: **practice a concrete action and then measure actual behavior.**

---

## Key takeaway

Fraud prevention requires more than awareness.

The intervention needs to change:

> **what happens in the few seconds after the telephone rings.**

In this field experiment:

- altruistic messaging; and
- immediate behavioral rehearsal

increased safer telephone-answering behavior one week later.

But the evidence also leaves clear next questions about persistence, nonresponders, allocation, generalizability, and actual fraud outcomes.

The EBP question should therefore move from:

> “Did the intervention work?”

to:

> **“Who still did not adopt the safer behavior, why not, and what intervention would close that remaining gap?”**

# Evaluating the Crime-Deterrent Effect of Hot-Spots Patrols in Japan

**Original study: Takahiro No & Eichi Itoigawa (2020)**

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent commentary prepared by the Research Hub to help police practitioners, crime prevention professionals, and other readers understand the study, its methodology, findings, limitations, and practical implications. It is not an explanation provided or endorsed by the original authors. Please consult the original publication for the complete methodology, results, and authors’ interpretations.

---

## What did the study do?

The study examined a direct operational question:

> **If police use crime data to identify high-risk places and concentrate patrol activity there, does recorded crime decrease?**

Four police jurisdictions in Nagano Prefecture were used.

The study:

1. identified high-risk locations from historical crime data;
2. concentrated police patrol in those locations;
3. recorded how much police activity actually occurred in the risk areas;
4. included comparison police jurisdictions without the concentrated-patrol intervention; and
5. compared recorded crime before and during the intervention.

Within the identified risk areas, statistically significant reductions (p<.05) were found for:

- vehicle-related theft during the day, burglary during the day and night, and sexual offences/related incidents at night in Station A; and
- sexual offences/related incidents during both day and night in Station B.

The importance of the study is therefore not simply that patrol was increased. It linked **target selection, implementation measurement, comparison areas, and crime outcomes** in one evaluation design.

---

## Why was the study conducted?

Police resources are limited.

The question of **where patrol time should be allocated** is therefore a resource-allocation decision, not merely a routine operational matter.

Crime is not evenly distributed across space. International research had already shown that crime tends to cluster in a relatively small number of places and that hot-spots policing can reduce crime.

Japan, however, differs from many settings in the international literature in crime volume, crime mix, urban environment, and police operations.

The study therefore tested place-based patrol under Japanese conditions rather than assuming that overseas evidence would transfer automatically.

---

## This study is part of a longer research sequence

The 2020 experiment was not an isolated project.

A 2017 study had already developed patrol routes from geographic crime-risk analysis and assessed their practical acceptability among frontline police officers.

The work then moved from:

**analysis and route design**
↓
**actual concentrated patrol**
↓
**crime-outcome evaluation**

A 2024 doctoral dissertation integrated the sequence into a broader methodology for place-based policing in Japan:

```text
identify high-risk places
        ↓
design patrol routes
        ↓
assess whether officers can use them
        ↓
implement patrol in the field
        ↓
evaluate crime outcomes
```

For the Research Hub, this progression from analysis to implementation and evaluation is itself an important EBP example.

---

## How were high-risk areas identified?

The study combined three main approaches:

- **hot-spot analysis**;
- **near-repeat victimization analysis**; and
- **area-characteristic analysis using Risk Terrain Modeling (RTM)**.

### Hot-spot analysis

Kernel density estimation was used to identify spatial concentrations of crime.

Rather than selecting thresholds entirely by analyst judgment, the study used the **Prediction Accuracy Index (PAI)** to compare bandwidths and historical data windows and select settings with stronger predictive performance.

### Near-repeat victimization

The study tested whether crimes were more likely to recur at nearby places within short time periods.

In Station A, significant near-repeat patterns were found for vehicle theft, burglary, and sexual offences/related incidents within **28 days and 500 metres**.

For example, daytime vehicle theft in August showed a Knox ratio of **2.26** for the same location within 8–14 days.

### Area characteristics

The study also tested relationships between crime and environmental features such as stations, car parks, hospitals, convenience stores, schools, parks, and other facilities.

For daytime sexual offences/related incidents in Station A, the estimated risk within 415 m of high schools was **4.7 times** the risk outside that area.

---

## How was the intervention evaluated?

The four Nagano police jurisdictions were organized as:

- **Stations A and B:** concentrated-patrol intervention
- **Stations C and D:** comparison jurisdictions

A was paired with C and B with D based on factors including population and recorded crime.

The authors characterize the design as **Level 3 on the Maryland Scientific Methods Scale**.

In practical terms, the study compared changes in intervention jurisdictions with changes in comparison jurisdictions rather than relying on a simple before-after comparison.

The design was not randomized.

The authors considered randomized allocation within police jurisdictions but, after discussion with practitioners, judged it inappropriate for crime-prevention operations and instead patrolled all identified risk areas in the intervention jurisdictions.

---

## How was patrol implemented?

The experiment ran for **three months, from August to October 2019**.

Key conditions included:

- risk areas up to approximately 500 m on a side;
- a target of three hours of presence in risk areas;
- uniformed patrol by foot, bicycle, or police vehicle;
- no additional staffing;
- daily recording of time in risk areas in 10-minute units; and
- recording of activity type and mode of movement.

Across all officers involved in the experiment, total daily activity averaged:

- **89 hours per day in Station A**
- **21 hours per day in Station B**

Police vehicles dominated the intervention:

- Station A: 87% of movement was by police vehicle and 91% of activity was patrol;
- Station B: 90% of movement was by police vehicle and 88% of activity was patrol.

This is important because the study measured **implementation dosage**, not simply whether a patrol order had been issued.

---

## What crime categories were examined?

Four broad categories were selected with police practitioners:

- vehicle-related theft;
- burglary;
- non-burglary theft; and
- sexual offences and related incidents.

For 2018, the intervention jurisdictions had different crime profiles:

| Crime category | Station A | Station B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Vehicle-related theft | 346 | 58 |
| Burglary | 120 | 39 |
| Non-burglary theft | 42 | 26 |
| Sexual offences / related incidents | 179 | 37 |

The same label—“hot-spots policing”—therefore covered quite different local crime problems.

---

## What did the study find?

### Jurisdiction-wide results varied by crime type

After removing seasonal variation using STL decomposition, the study compared the three months immediately before the intervention with the three-month intervention period.

For Station A versus Station C:

- vehicle-related theft changed **8 percentage points more favourably** in A;
- burglary changed **31 percentage points more favourably** in A;
- non-burglary theft increased in both, but the increase was **67 percentage points lower** in A;
- sexual offences/related incidents increased in A while decreasing in C.

For Station B versus Station D:

- vehicle-related theft increased **21 percentage points more** in B;
- sexual offences/related incidents fell **51%** in B while remaining almost unchanged in D.

The jurisdiction-wide pattern was therefore heterogeneous.

### Within risk areas, several crime categories declined significantly

In locations that remained risk areas across all three intervention months, statistically significant reductions (p<.05) were found in:

Station A:
- daytime vehicle-related theft;
- daytime burglary;
- nighttime burglary;
- nighttime sexual offences/related incidents.

Station B:
- daytime sexual offences/related incidents;
- nighttime sexual offences/related incidents.

The authors therefore conclude that concentrated patrol may have deterrent effects for specific crime types under these conditions.

## Check the statistics


### What was compared?

The study evaluated outcomes at two main levels.

#### 1. Whole police jurisdictions

```text
Station A  vs  Station C
Station B  vs  Station D
```

Changes in recorded crime during the three-month intervention were compared with the immediately preceding three months.

To reduce seasonal confounding, crime time series from 2016–2019 were decomposed using STL and seasonal variation was removed.

#### 2. Identified risk areas

The authors identified locations that were classified as risk areas in all three intervention months and compared crime counts in those places between:

- the previous three months; and
- the three-month intervention period.

Because risk-area crime counts contained many zeroes and the mean and variance differed substantially, the study used the **Wilcoxon signed-rank test**, a non-parametric method.

---

### Crime counts within risk areas

#### Station A

| Crime category | Previous 3 months Mean (SD) | Intervention 3 months Mean (SD) | Result |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Vehicle theft – day | 1.6 (2.4) | 0.3 (0.4) | **p<.05** |
| Burglary – day | 0.4 (0.6) | 0.1 (0.2) | **p<.05** |
| Non-burglary theft – day | 0.5 (0.5) | 0 (0) | n.s. |
| Sexual offences etc. – day | 0.2 (0.4) | 0 (0) | n.s. |
| Vehicle theft – night | 1.2 (2.7) | 0.9 (0.4) | n.s. |
| Burglary – night | 0.4 (0.5) | 0 (0) | **p<.05** |
| Non-burglary theft – night | 0.1 (0.4) | 0.1 (0.3) | n.s. |
| Sexual offences etc. – night | 0.4 (0.7) | 0 (0) | **p<.05** |

#### Station B

| Crime category | Previous 3 months Mean (SD) | Intervention 3 months Mean (SD) | Result |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Vehicle theft – day | 0.3 (0.6) | 0 (0) | n.s. |
| Sexual offences etc. – day | 0.6 (0.7) | 0 (0) | **p<.05** |
| Vehicle theft – night | 0.6 (0.5) | 0.1 (0.4) | n.s. |
| Sexual offences etc. – night | 0.7 (0.5) | 0 (0) | **p<.05** |

`n.s.` means that the difference was not statistically significant.

---

### How should these numbers be read?

For daytime vehicle theft in Station A, the mean fell from:

**1.6 to 0.3**

and the before-after difference was statistically significant at p<.05.

By contrast, daytime non-burglary theft in Station A fell from:

**0.5 to 0**

but the statistical test did not establish a clear difference.

This distinction matters:

> **a lower observed number is not automatically the same as a statistically supported intervention effect.**

The same applies to vehicle theft in Station B: the mean fell, but the difference was not statistically clear.

---

### Implementation dosage also matters

Average total patrol activity differed substantially:

- Station A: **89 officer-hours per day**
- Station B: **21 officer-hours per day**

The authors note that differences between jurisdictions may reflect variation in crime volume, local environment, and time spent in risk areas for specific offences.

The label “concentrated patrol” therefore does not imply identical intervention dosage.

---

### Was there displacement or diffusion of benefits?

The study created buffers of:

- 50 m;
- 100 m; and
- 200 m

around risk areas.

Across Stations A and B, **no statistically significant changes were found for any crime category in the buffer analyses**.

Within the 200 m buffer in Station A:

- nighttime burglary decreased;
- nighttime non-burglary theft increased.

The authors describe these only as possible diffusion of benefits and possible displacement, respectively.

The evidence was not statistically strong enough to conclude that either process definitely occurred.

---

### What did officers think?

Pre/post surveys involved:

- **118 officers in Station A**
- **88 officers in Station B**

Post-intervention support rates were:

| Item | Station A | Station B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Risk maps are necessary | 81% | 83% |
| Concentrated patrol has a deterrent effect | 92% | 95% |
| It contributes to citizen reassurance | 70% | 73% |
| Technical support can reduce work time | 52% | 38% |

The pattern is notable:

**officers strongly supported the deterrence value of targeted patrol, but did not strongly believe that it reduced workload.**

Operational effectiveness and operational efficiency are not the same outcome.

---

### What did the statistics help decompose?

The study was not simply:

```text
high-crime place
      ↓
more patrol
      ↓
crime fell
```

It examined:

```text
historical crime analysis
      ↓
risk-area identification
      ↓
targeted police deployment
      ↓
measurement of implementation
      ↓
jurisdiction-level comparison
      ↓
risk-area comparison
      ↓
displacement/diffusion analysis
      ↓
officer acceptability
```

The statistics therefore help identify **which outcomes changed, which did not, and under which local conditions the intervention appeared to work**.

## How should the findings be interpreted?

### What the study directly establishes

The study:

- identified high-risk places using geographic crime analysis;
- implemented actual concentrated police patrol;
- measured police activity;
- used comparison police jurisdictions;
- evaluated outcomes both across jurisdictions and within risk areas;
- found statistically significant reductions for several crime/time combinations; and
- examined possible displacement and diffusion.

### What the results reasonably suggest

Rather than distributing police resources uniformly, Japanese police agencies may benefit from testing strategies that:

**identify concentrated risk → target patrol → measure implementation → compare outcomes.**

The study also demonstrates that crime analysis can be connected directly to operational deployment and evaluation.

### What the study cannot establish on its own

It does not provide a universal answer for:

- optimal patrol duration;
- optimal frequency of visits;
- which patrol mode is most effective;
- how long effects persist;
- whether findings generalize to other prefectures or cities; or
- the optimal dosage for each crime type.

---

## Limitations and alternative explanations

The authors identify several important limitations.

### 1. Police patrol was not the only possible influence

Citizen crime-prevention activity may also have affected crime in the risk areas.

Even though police activity was recorded, the observed reduction cannot be attributed exclusively to police patrol.

### 2. Police activity outside risk areas was not measured

This limits the precision of the displacement and diffusion analysis.

### 3. Optimal patrol dosage was not identified

The study set target presence times but did not determine the best duration, frequency, or patrol mode.

### 4. The experiment lasted three months and was not randomized

The authors explicitly describe the evidence as limited under these conditions and call for further experiments to test robustness.

# Research Hub commentary: the main lesson is not simply “patrol works”

Reducing the study to:

> hot-spots patrol works

would miss much of its practical value.

The more important sequence is:

```text
Where is the problem concentrated?
      ↓
Where should police resources be targeted?
      ↓
Can frontline officers use the method?
      ↓
Does deployment actually change?
      ↓
Was implementation recorded?
      ↓
Did the target outcome change?
      ↓
Did crime move elsewhere?
      ↓
Should deployment be adjusted again?
```

That is a practical form of evidence-based policing.

The distinctive feature is that **analysis changed police deployment and the consequences of that deployment were then evaluated with outcome data**.

---

## The study can also be read through the SARA model

The authors explicitly describe a process resembling:

- **Scanning**: identify local crime problems;
- **Analysis**: examine target offences using risk maps;
- **Response**: conduct concentrated patrol in risk areas; and
- **Assessment**: examine whether crime problems changed.

The study can therefore be read not only as hot-spots policing but also as an example of **Problem-Oriented Policing / SARA supported by data and evaluation**.

---

## How could practitioners use this approach?

For a local bicycle-theft problem:

```text
map theft concentrations
      ↓
identify time-place clusters
      ↓
select intervention and comparison areas
      ↓
record patrol visits and time spent
      ↓
track theft and locking outcomes
      ↓
compare changes
```

The number of patrols is an implementation measure.

The core operational question is whether the targeted problem changed after deployment.

---

## What should be measured next?

A stronger replication could record:

1. crime counts by micro-place;
2. officer time spent in each target area;
3. patrol frequency;
4. patrol mode and activity type;
5. time of day;
6. crime-specific outcomes;
7. crime in adjacent areas;
8. persistence after the intervention;
9. officer workload and operational efficiency; and
10. citizen reassurance.

This would move the evidence from:

> targeted patrol appears to reduce crime

toward:

> **what amount and type of police activity reduces which crime, in which places, and for how long?**

## Key takeaway

Hot-spots policing is not evidence-based merely because police concentrate patrol in a high-crime area.

The EBP sequence is:

**use data to choose the target**
↓
**change police deployment**
↓
**measure implementation**
↓
**use a comparison**
↓
**measure crime outcomes**
↓
**feed the findings back into the next deployment**

This research program is important because it tested that sequence within Japanese policing.

# Police Attitudes, Victim Experience, and Investigative Interviewing in Sexual-Offence Cases
## Reading the association between perceived appropriate treatment, information provision, and victims’ sense of relief

**Original study: Hirama, Yokota, Wachi, Watanabe, Furuhashi, Otsuka & Shimada (2026)**

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent commentary prepared by the Research Hub to help police practitioners, crime prevention professionals, and other readers understand the study, its methodology, findings, limitations, and practical implications. It is not an explanation provided or endorsed by the original authors. Please consult the original publication for the complete methodology, results, and authors’ interpretations.

---

## What did the study examine?

The study examined how police officers’ attitudes during interviews with victims of sexual offences were perceived by both victims and officers, and how those perceptions were associated with three outcomes:

- how fully victims felt they had communicated information about the offence;
- whether officers felt they had obtained information needed for the investigation; and
- victims’ sense of relief after dealing with the police.

A distinctive feature is that the analysis used **matched victim–officer pairs** rather than surveying only one side of the interaction.

The final analysis included **122 pairs, or 244 people**.

This was not an experiment in which interviewing behaviour was randomly assigned.

Despite the wording “effects” in the article title, the design should be read as an analysis of **associations in post-interview survey data**, not as a causal test of a training programme or interviewing intervention.

---

## Why does this matter?

Sexual-offence investigations require police to obtain accurate information while avoiding unnecessary secondary harm to victims.

These objectives may sometimes be treated as competing demands:

> obtain detailed information for the investigation  
> versus  
> reduce psychological burden and treat the victim with care.

The study draws on **procedural justice**, which concerns whether people feel that they were heard, respected, treated with dignity and dealt with fairly and honestly.

The practical question was therefore whether respectful and considerate treatment is merely a victim-support issue, or whether it is also associated with the quality of the investigative interaction.

---

## How was the study conducted?

The eligible cases were sexual-offence cases recorded by prefectural police forces between October 2017 and December 2018, using the offence classifications in force at the time.

Victims had to have been aged 18 or older at the time of the offence, and the suspect had to have been arrested and referred for prosecution.

The final sample contained **122 matched victim–officer pairs**.

Among victims, 116 (95.1%) were women and the mean age was 27.70 years. Among officers, 63 (51.6%) were women and the mean age was 34.49 years.

Victims and officers completed corresponding items about the officer’s behaviour, including:

- consideration for the victim’s feelings;
- efforts to understand the victim;
- reducing anxiety;
- creating a setting in which it was easier to talk;
- sincerity;
- careful listening; and
- commitment to the investigation.

Items on impersonal treatment and an attitude that victims “should naturally cooperate” were also included.

Factor analysis produced a common dimension interpreted as **appropriate attitude**.

The study then used an Actor–Partner Interdependence Model (APIM) to examine how the victim’s perception and the officer’s self-perception related to outcomes reported by each member of the pair.

---

## What did the study find?

### Victims and officers generally rated the behaviour positively

For seven considerate-behaviour items, mean scores exceeded 4.5 on the five-point scale for both groups.

The two negatively framed items had means below 2.2.

Both victim and officer responses produced a one-factor solution interpreted as appropriate attitude.

However, the victim and officer factor scores were not strongly identical. The intraclass correlation was .12, with a 95% confidence interval spanning zero.

This matters because **an officer’s view of having acted appropriately is not interchangeable with the victim’s experience of that interaction**.

### Victims’ perceptions were associated with both information and well-being outcomes

When victims perceived the officer’s attitude as more appropriate, they also reported being better able to provide information about their victimisation.

The officers paired with those victims were also more likely to report having obtained information needed for the investigation.

Victims’ perceptions of appropriate treatment were additionally associated with a stronger sense of relief.

### Officers’ self-perceptions were also associated with victims’ relief

Officers who rated their own behaviour as more appropriate were paired with victims who reported a higher sense of relief.

However, indirect pathways in which appropriate treatment increased relief *through* information provision were not statistically clear.

The study therefore does not support a single simple mechanism such as:

> better treatment → more information → relief.

## Check the statistics

### What was the analytical sample?

The target allocation was 304 cases. Questionnaires were returned by 194 victims and 246 officers, but unmatched cases, cases without the required consent, and cases with missing analytical variables were excluded.

The final analytical sample was **122 matched pairs**.

This means the findings apply to a selected set of cases that were reported to police, involved an arrested and referred suspect, and had participation from both victim and officer.

### How was “appropriate attitude” measured?

Categorical factor analysis produced one factor on each side.

Internal consistency was high:

- officers: **α = .87**
- victims: **α = .94**

The victim–officer intraclass correlation for the factor score was:

**ICC = .12, 95% CI [-.06, .29]**

The authors describe the magnitude as moderate by the criterion they used, but the confidence interval includes zero.

It would therefore be too strong to say that victim and officer ratings were statistically proven to be closely aligned.

### Main APIM associations

Higher **victim-perceived appropriate attitude** was associated with:

- victim-reported provision of victimisation information: **β = .66, SE = .09, p < .001**
- officer-reported acquisition of necessary investigative information: **β = .30, SE = .12, p = .015**
- victim sense of relief: **β = .70, SE = .09, p < .001**

Higher **officer self-rated appropriate attitude** was associated with:

- victim sense of relief: **β = .21, SE = .11, p = .046**

These are standardised coefficients. They are not percentages.

For example, β=.66 does **not** mean that information provision increased by 66%.

### What about mediation?

Indirect effects through information provision or information acquisition were not statistically clear because the confidence intervals included zero.

The study therefore does not establish that information exchange is the mechanism by which considerate treatment improves victims’ sense of relief.

### Where to verify this in the article

- **Table 1**: attitude items, agreement measures and factor loadings
- **Figure 1**: APIM paths and standardised coefficients
- **Methods**: case eligibility and reduction to 122 pairs
- **Discussion**: interpretation and methodological limitations

## How should the findings be interpreted?

### Directly supported

Victims who perceived more appropriate treatment also reported better information provision and a greater sense of relief. Their paired officers also reported greater acquisition of necessary investigative information.

### Reasonably suggested

Victim care and investigative information-gathering should not automatically be treated as opposing objectives.

The findings also suggest that evaluation should include the **victim’s perception of police behaviour**, rather than relying only on officer self-assessment.

### Not established

The study does not show that:

- a particular training programme causes better information provision;
- a specific interview technique causes better victim well-being; or
- appropriate treatment increases case-clearance or prosecution outcomes.

---

## Limitations and alternative explanations

### Participation bias

The authors note that victims who agreed to participate may have held relatively favourable views of the police.

### Subjective outcome measures

Information provision and information acquisition were measured through participants’ own assessments rather than objective coding of information quantity or investigative value.

### Response-scale design

The verbal labels and numerical direction of some response scales were inconsistent, creating a potential source of response bias.

### Case progression may still matter

The analysis adjusted for whether a case was not prosecuted and for some officer–victim demographic combinations, but the influence of case progress and outcomes cannot be completely removed.

### Restricted case scope

The study concerned reported cases in which a suspect had been arrested and referred. It cannot directly tell us whether the same relationships apply to unreported cases or cases without an identified suspect.

# Research Hub commentary: do not separate “victim care” and “investigation” too sharply

A useful practical reading is that respectful treatment is not merely an additional service placed beside the investigation.

A plausible process is:

```text
Officer behaves considerately
        ↓
Victim experiences the interaction as respectful and safe
        ↓
The victim may find it easier to communicate
        ↓
The investigative interaction may become more productive
```

This causal chain was **not experimentally demonstrated** in the study.

However, the observed results do not support a simple assumption that greater care necessarily impedes information gathering.

The next EBP step is to translate “appropriate attitude” into observable and trainable behaviours, then test whether those behaviours can be improved and whether victim and investigative outcomes change.

Potential behaviours might include:

- listening without unnecessary interruption;
- explaining why sensitive questions are being asked;
- allowing meaningful opportunities to speak;
- avoiding judgmental responses;
- explaining the next procedural steps; and
- checking the victim’s understanding and preferences.

---

## What could practitioners measure in a local evaluation?

A training or procedural-change evaluation could separately measure:

- whether victims felt respected and able to talk;
- discrepancies between officer self-ratings and victim ratings;
- objective quantity and relevance of information obtained;
- victim-reported secondary harm;
- later willingness to cooperate with police;
- whether trained behaviours were actually used in interviews.

The EBP sequence would be:

**training → observable interviewing behaviour → victim perception → information and well-being outcomes.**

---

## Related concept: procedural justice

Procedural justice focuses on how people experience the process of police decision-making and interaction, including voice, dignity, respect and fairness.

This study applies that perspective to a highly sensitive area of investigative practice.

---

## Key takeaway

**Victim care and investigative effectiveness should not automatically be framed as a trade-off.**

In this study, victims’ perceptions of appropriate police treatment were positively associated with:

- their own assessment of information provision;
- officers’ assessment of obtaining necessary investigative information; and
- victims’ sense of relief.

But the study is observational.

The next question is whether specific, trainable police behaviours can causally improve these outcomes.

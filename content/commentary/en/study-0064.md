# Can School Crime-Prevention Programmes Reduce Parental Fear?
## Reading an apparently paradoxical result without turning association into causation

> **About this commentary**  
> This commentary is based on the publisher's public abstract, bibliographic information and the Research Hub's verified study record. The full article is subscription-only. Programme-specific coefficients or statistics that could not be checked in the full text are therefore not reproduced here.

---

## What did the study examine?

The study asked whether exposure to school-based crime-prevention programmes was associated with mothers' **fear of strangers**.

An online survey included **275 mothers in Tokyo with daughters in Grades 3–6 of primary school**. Multiple regression models examined associations between programme implementation or participation and maternal fear while controlling for parent, child and neighbourhood factors.

This was a **cross-sectional observational study**, not a randomised trial.

---

## What was found?

The public abstract reports that:

- no programme was associated with lower fear of strangers; and
- three school-based crime-prevention programmes were statistically associated with higher maternal fear.

The tempting interpretation is that crime-prevention education increased fear. The design does not establish that causal claim.

---

## Why is reverse causation important?

The same pattern could arise if:

- families who were already more concerned about safety were more likely to participate;
- schools or neighbourhoods with greater concern implemented more programmes; or
- prior local events influenced both programme exposure and fear.

Thus, both pathways remain plausible:

**programme exposure → fear**

and

**higher pre-existing fear → programme exposure**.

Cross-sectional data cannot securely establish the temporal order.

## How should practitioners read this?

The study supports a broader evaluation lesson: **crime-prevention education can have multiple outcomes**.

A useful evaluation can separately measure:

- children's safety knowledge;
- protective behaviour;
- parental fear and anxiety;
- parental restrictions on children's activities; and
- children's independent mobility.

The study does not show that the programmes caused fear, reduced crime or changed victimisation.

---

# Research Hub commentary: measure unintended outcomes as well as intended ones

Crime-prevention education can plausibly affect a chain of outcomes:

programme → attention to danger → risk perception → fear → protective or avoidant behaviour → children's mobility.

EBP should not automatically classify higher fear as either a failure or evidence that participants "understood the risk". The practical task is to measure intended and unintended effects together.

---

## Take-away

**Evaluate safety and fear separately.**

The study found positive associations between exposure to some programmes and maternal fear, but it did not establish that the programmes caused the fear. For practice, the main lesson is to include fear, behavioural restriction and other possible unintended outcomes in evaluation designs.

# Is Feeling Safe Enough?
## A resident survey as the Analysis stage of evidence-based problem solving

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent Research Hub commentary based on the full article published by Nihon University College of Risk Management.

---

## What did the project do?

Tsuzuki Police Station sought resident data after increases in cybercrime and street-crime concerns, including bicycle theft and molestation. Nihon University staff and eight students worked with police on question design, analysis and feedback to the police station.

This is not primarily an intervention-effect study. Its practical role is **problem analysis before response selection**.

---

## Three relevant samples

The online survey used customers of OK Net Supermarket:

- Survey 1: **145 respondents** on perceived safety, general crime risk and cybercrime;
- bicycle-theft component: **81 respondents**; and
- molestation component: **28 women**.

The differing denominators matter when interpreting findings.

---

## What did the survey reveal?

Perceived neighbourhood safety was relatively high, averaging **5.77 on a seven-point scale**. At the same time, respondents showed positive optimism bias regarding crime risk: a tendency to see their own risk as lower than that of comparable others.

Respondents also tended to believe that several crime-prevention activities were effective. Those perceptions should not be confused with measured crime-reduction effects.

Survey findings were subsequently reported back to the police-station council and used in discussion of future local responses.

## Check the statistics

- Survey 1: n = 145
- Bicycle-theft survey: n = 81
- Molestation survey: n = 28 women
- Tsuzuki residents in Survey 1: 78
- Nearby-area residents: 67

The article reports no significant differences between those two residence groups on the main perceived-safety variables examined, after which the data were combined.

Because the study is cross-sectional, regression relationships are interpreted here as **associations**, not causal effects.

## Why this matters for EBP

Evidence-based practice does not start with an intervention. It starts by specifying the problem.

A resident may:

- feel the area is safe;
- underestimate personal risk;
- believe a prevention measure works; and
- still engage in risky behaviour.

These are separate constructs. Measuring them can help distinguish whether the next response should target knowledge, risk perception, behaviour or the environment.

---

## Limitations

The sample was a convenience sample of online-supermarket users rather than a random sample of all residents. Outcomes were self-reported and cross-sectional. Some crime-specific subsamples were small. The study did not evaluate whether a later police intervention reduced crime.

---

# Research Hub commentary: separate safety, reassurance and behaviour

The key practical lesson is to avoid using a single question such as "Do residents feel safe?" as the whole diagnosis. Analysis should separate objective crime patterns, subjective safety, risk perception, knowledge and behaviour before choosing a response.

---

## Take-away

**Measure the problem before evaluating the solution.**

The police–university collaboration is valuable as an example of using resident data to structure the Analysis stage and feed findings back into local police planning.

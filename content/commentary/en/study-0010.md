# Convenience-Store Robbery and Store Environment
## Using victim/non-victim comparisons to generate intervention hypotheses

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent Research Hub commentary based on the cited source material. It distinguishes reported findings, authors’ interpretations and Research Hub commentary, and should be read alongside the original source.


The study analyzed 79 robbery incidents at 74 convenience stores in Fukuoka and paired each victim store with a nearby non-victim store, producing **148 stores** for field observation.

Researchers measured 51 store and surrounding-environment variables during the high-risk midnight–06:00 period.

The study belongs to the **Analysis** stage of EBP rather than the impact-evaluation stage.

## Check the statistics

Victim stores showed poorer visibility as a trend:
- p=.068

Average parking capacity:
- good visibility: 9.3 spaces
- poor visibility: 14.2

Within victim stores, larger parking capacity correlated with:
- fewer lights: r=-.280
- poorer visibility: r=.259
- absence of pedestrians: r=.560
- absence of bicycles: r=.331
- commercial zoning: r=-.684
- number of shops: r=-.572
- other convenience stores: r=-.405

Poor visibility correlated with:
- fewer lights: r=-.558
- fewer road lanes: r=-.331
- absence of bicycles: r=.418
- absence of motorcycles: r=.370
- absence of cars: r=.351
- greater distance to station: r=.443

These are correlations and cannot be interpreted as treatment effects.

## How should the findings be interpreted?

The value is to identify **modifiable candidates**—visibility, layout, staff behavior and guardianship—for later intervention testing.

The study does not establish that changing one of these features will reduce robbery by a known percentage.

Incident records also showed a strong association between employee initial action and attempted versus completed robbery, but this should not be translated into advice to physically resist armed offenders.

## Research Hub commentary

A useful EBP sequence is:

**compare victim and non-victim settings → identify modifiable features → intervene → evaluate.**

Fixed contextual factors such as land-use zoning may help explain risk, but they are less actionable than lighting, visibility, layout and routine staff behavior.

## Key takeaway

Separate **modifiable environmental features** from contextual correlates, then test the modifiable ones prospectively.

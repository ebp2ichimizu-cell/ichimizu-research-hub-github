# Experimental Study of Crime-Prevention Information Dissemination by a Public Agency
## Implementation and evaluation based on Protection Motivation Theory

**Original study: Shirakawa, Shimada & Higuchi (2023)**

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent commentary prepared by the Research Hub to help police practitioners, crime prevention professionals, and other readers understand the study, its methodology, findings, limitations, and practical implications. It is not an explanation provided or endorsed by the original authors. Please consult the original publication for the complete methodology, results, and authors’ interpretations.

---

## What did the study do?

The study examined whether crime-prevention information disseminated through the Metropolitan Police Department's official Twitter account could change intentions and actual preventive behavior related to communications fraud.

Participants were shown information in stages about:

- the threat of fraud;
- the effectiveness of preventive actions; and
- self-efficacy, or the belief that the preventive action could be carried out.

Three behaviors were examined:

- establishing a family password;
- using or recommending an answering-machine function; and
- checking the identity of callers.

The findings were not uniform. Some measures of behavioral intention and perceived effectiveness changed, but **actual preventive behavior did not show clear differences between conditions over time**.

The key practical lesson is therefore not that public information “worked” or “failed.” The evaluation separated the pathway from information exposure to intention and then to action, making the next bottleneck visible.

---

## Why was the study conducted?

Police and public agencies frequently use websites, leaflets, email, social media, lectures, and other channels to communicate crime-prevention advice.

But delivering information is not the same as changing behavior.

A person may:

- understand the risk;
- believe a preventive measure is useful; and
- intend to act,

without actually carrying out that behavior.

The study therefore applied **Protection Motivation Theory (PMT)** to examine different stages in this pathway.

A simplified version is:

```text
Perceive the threat
      ↓
Believe the response is effective
      ↓
Believe “I can do it”
      ↓
Form an intention
      ↓
Take action
```

---

## How was the experiment conducted?

Participants in their 20s to 50s who had parents or grandparents were randomly assigned to two conditions.

### Metropolitan Police Department condition

Participants viewed fraud-prevention tweets from the Metropolitan Police Department.

Information was added progressively:

```text
Threat information
      ↓
Threat + response effectiveness
      ↓
Threat + response effectiveness + self-efficacy
```

### Control condition

Participants viewed tweets from the Fire and Disaster Management Agency concerning topics other than fraud prevention.

Measures were collected at six time points and included:

- behavioral intention;
- perceived response effectiveness;
- self-efficacy;
- fear; and
- actual preventive behavior.

A total of 177 people were randomized: 86 to the police condition and 91 to the control condition. After exclusions based on the study's analytic criteria, the main analysis included **60 in the police condition and 49 in the control condition**.

---

## What did the study find?

### Behavioral intention changed for some behaviors

For use of an answering-machine function, behavioral intention was higher in the police condition.

For establishing a family password, a difference emerged at the follow-up time point.

For checking the caller's identity, a clear condition effect was not established.

### Perceived effectiveness also changed

For some preventive actions, particularly answering-machine use and caller checking, participants in the police condition rated the measures as more effective.

### Actual behavior did not show a clear intervention effect

For all three preventive behaviors, the study did not find clear differences in implementation rates by condition or measurement period.

This creates an important distinction:

**changing what people think or intend to do is not the same as changing what they actually do.**

## Check the statistics


### Sample and design

Of 177 randomized participants, the main analysis included:

- **60 in the Metropolitan Police Department condition**
- **49 in the control condition**

Behavioral intention and related constructs were rated on 1–7 scales across six measurement points. The paper used two-factor analyses of variance and adjusted the significance criterion to address multiple testing across the three preventive behaviors.

### Family password

At follow-up (T6), mean behavioral-intention scores were:

- **Police condition: 5.20**
- **Control condition: 4.24**

The condition-by-time interaction was:

**F(4.44, 439.74) = 2.97, p < .05, partial η² = .03**

and the condition difference at T6 was:

**F(1, 99) = 10.17, p < .01, partial η² = .09**

### Answering-machine use

At T6:

- **Police condition: 5.40**
- **Control condition: 4.67**

The main effect of condition was:

**F(1, 99) = 7.29, p < .05, partial η² = .07**

### Caller checking

The main effect of condition was not statistically clear:

**F(1, 99) = 4.12, ns, partial η² = .04**

### Actual behavior at T6

| Behavior | Police condition | Control condition |
|---|---:|---:|
| Established a family password | 19% | 15% |
| Used/recommended answering-machine function | 33% | 22% |
| Used caller-checking measures | 44% | 37% |

Although these descriptive percentages differ, the original analysis found **no clear condition or time effect on actual implementation rates**.

### What do the statistics add?

They reveal a staged pattern:

```text
Information presented
      ↓
Some intentions changed
      ↓
Some perceived effectiveness measures changed
      ↓
Actual behavior did not show a clear intervention effect
```

This turns the next question from:

> “Did the communication work?”

into:

> **“What prevented increased intention from becoming action?”**

For further verification, see **Table 1, Table 3, and Table 5**, and the Results/Discussion sections on pp. 520–524 of the original article.

## How should the findings be interpreted?

The randomized comparison gives more leverage than a simple before-and-after survey for examining the effect of information exposure.

However, the findings differ by outcome. It would be too broad to say that “crime-prevention communication increased behavior.”

A better interpretation is:

- some psychological precursors moved;
- the pattern differed by preventive action; and
- actual behavior did not show a clear intervention effect.

The study also did not measure fraud victimization itself, so it cannot establish that the communication reduced fraud losses.

---

## Limitations and alternative explanations

The experimental setting differed from ordinary social-media use because participants were instructed to view specified accounts.

Because real Twitter accounts were used, exposure could not be perfectly controlled.

Repeated measurement itself may also have increased attention to fraud prevention in both groups.

There was substantial attrition from randomization to the final analytic sample, which should be kept in mind when interpreting the randomized comparison.

Finally, participants were adults in their 20s to 50s with parents or grandparents, not elderly victims themselves.

# Research Hub commentary: use evaluation to locate the behavioral bottleneck

The practical message is not that public communication is insufficient.

Without evaluation, practitioners may see only the final outcome:

> preventive behavior did or did not increase.

This study decomposes that outcome:

```text
Did the information reach people?
      ↓
Did they understand the risk?
      ↓
Did they believe the response was effective?
      ↓
Did intention increase?
      ↓
Did actual behavior change?
```

Here, some intermediate stages changed while actual behavior did not show a clear intervention effect.

That makes the next problem more precise.

Future interventions can investigate the **intention-to-action gap**, for example by testing whether implementation becomes easier when people are given immediate instructions, direct links, practical assistance, reminders, or other mechanisms that reduce friction.

Those mechanisms were not tested in this study. Their value as hypotheses comes from the fact that evaluation narrowed the location of the problem.

---

## What should practitioners measure?

A local evaluation could separately assess:

1. reach;
2. understanding;
3. perceived risk;
4. perceived response effectiveness;
5. self-efficacy;
6. behavioral intention;
7. actual preventive behavior; and
8. victimization or prevented incidents, where feasible.

This helps identify where the pathway stops rather than treating communication as a single undifferentiated intervention.

---

## Related theory

### Protection Motivation Theory

PMT explains preventive behavior through threat appraisal and coping appraisal, including response effectiveness and self-efficacy.

The study is useful because it measured several stages in that process rather than treating “awareness” as the final outcome.

---

## Key takeaway

**Evaluation is not only a test of whether a campaign succeeds or fails.**

It can reveal which stage changes and which stage does not.

In this study, some intentions and beliefs moved, while actual behavior did not show a clear intervention effect.

That evidence turns a broad problem into a more testable one:

**understand the pathway → identify where it stalls → redesign the intervention → test again.**

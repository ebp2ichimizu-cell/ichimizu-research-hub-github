# Identifying Bottlenecks in Bicycle Locking and Testing a Poster Intervention
## A three-stage problem-solving process that goes beyond “the poster did not work”

**Original study: Oi et al. (2025)**

> **About this commentary**  
> This page is an independent commentary prepared by the Research Hub to help police practitioners, crime prevention professionals, and other readers understand the study, its methodology, findings, limitations, and practical implications. It is not an explanation provided or endorsed by the original authors. Please consult the original publication for the complete methodology, results, and authors’ interpretations.

---

## What did the study do?

This study addressed bicycle theft in Kochi Prefecture through a three-stage process:

**identify the problem → identify direct behavioral determinants → test an intervention aimed at those determinants.**

First, the researchers showed that unlocked bicycles at a railway station were about **14 times more likely to be stolen** than locked bicycles.

Second, interviews and school surveys identified two direct determinants of locking behavior:

- **habit**; and
- **perceived inconvenience or hassle**.

Third, the researchers designed posters intended to activate locking habits or reduce perceived hassle and tested them using a cluster randomized controlled trial.

The intervention did not produce a clear increase in locking rates.

However, the implementation analysis showed that many students **did not notice the posters, and many who did notice them could not accurately recall the content**.

The study is therefore more informative than the simple conclusion that “posters do not work.” It identified a new bottleneck: the intervention may not have reached the intended audience strongly enough to test the behavioral mechanism.

---

## Stage 1: where is the problem and how large is the risk?

Using 2022 open crime data, the researchers identified the area around JR Kochi Station as a major bicycle-theft location.

An observational survey in March 2024 recorded:

- 889 locked bicycles; and
- 130 unlocked bicycles.

The analysis indicated that an unlocked bicycle was approximately 14 times more likely to be stolen than a locked bicycle.

The study therefore began by moving beyond the general statement that “unlocked bicycles are a problem” and quantified the risk in a relevant location.

---

## Stage 2: why do people fail to lock bicycles?

The researchers then used interviews—primarily with secondary-school students—and a larger school survey to examine reasons for locking and not locking.

The survey included 3 junior-high schools and 5 high schools, with 3,806 students.

Factor analysis and structural modeling identified two particularly direct influences on locking:

### Habit

Locking the bicycle as an automatic or routine behavior.

### Perceived hassle

Experiencing locking as inconvenient or burdensome.

The problem was therefore not explained only by lack of risk awareness.

---

## Stage 3: testing a poster intervention

The researchers created three poster conditions.

### Habit cue

A message designed to remind students of habitual locking.

### Hassle-reduction message

A message framing locking as quick and easy.

### Control

A general bicycle-locking message.

Conditions were assigned by school or grade-level clusters, and the posters were displayed in school bicycle parking areas for 30 days.

The intervention involved eight schools, approximately 4,039 students in the survey frame, and about 2,300 bicycles.

Researchers also directly observed locking before the intervention, three days after the intervention began, and after 30 days.

---

## What did the study find?

### 1. No clear intervention effect on locking rate

Changes in locking rates varied roughly from -12 to +3 percentage points across schools and periods, with no consistent intervention pattern.

Difference-in-differences analyses did not identify a clear effect of the habit or hassle-reduction poster conditions.

### 2. Many students did not notice the posters

This is the critical implementation finding.

At 30 days, **56%** of respondents did not recognize the posters.

At 60 days, **81%** did not recognize them.

Moreover, most respondents did not accurately remember the poster content: 86% at 30 days and 97% at 60 days.

This means that before concluding that the behavioral message itself was ineffective, practitioners need to ask whether the message was actually received.

### 3. Poster recognition may have been concentrated among people already inclined to lock

Poster recognition was positively associated with locking habit.

The authors therefore note that the messages may not have adequately reached those who lacked a locking habit or perceived locking as burdensome—the very groups the intervention was intended to influence.

## Check the statistics

> The main text remains readable without statistics, while readers who want to verify the evidence can expand this section.

### The analysis proceeded in stages

The study did not rely on a single statistical test.

It first quantified theft risk, then identified behavioral determinants, and finally tested an intervention aimed at those determinants.

At the intervention stage, observed locking-rate changes ranged from approximately **-12 to +3 percentage points**, with no systematic pattern.

The questionnaire data were then analyzed using repeated difference-in-differences (DID).

For the outcome “always locks,” the key interaction coefficients were:

- **30 days × habit-cue intervention: 0.0192 (SE 0.0665)**
- **30 days × hassle-reduction intervention: -0.0117 (SE 0.0703)**

Neither provided clear statistical evidence of an intervention effect.

### What does that mean?

DID asks whether the change in an intervention condition is larger than the change in the comparison condition.

The analysis therefore guards against treating any simple before-after change as evidence caused by the poster.

---

### Did people who noticed the poster lock more?

When poster recognition was added to the model, it was positively associated with always locking:

**coefficient 0.0511, SE 0.019, p < .01.**

But after adding pre-existing locking habit and perceived hassle, the poster-recognition coefficient fell to:

**0.00219 (SE 0.0167),**

and was no longer statistically clear.

In the same model:

- **habit: 0.175, p < .001**
- **hassle: -0.0460, p < .001**

were strongly associated with locking behavior.

### Why is this important?

A simple comparison could suggest:

> people who noticed the poster were more likely to lock.

The expanded model shows an alternative explanation:

> people who already had stronger locking habits and experienced less hassle may have been both more likely to notice the poster and more likely to lock.

This is a practical example of why an observed association should not automatically be interpreted as an intervention effect.

---

### Reach and recognition

At 30 days:

- **56% had not noticed the poster.**

At 60 days:

- **81% had not noticed it.**

The proportions who could not accurately recall the content were:

- **86% at 30 days**
- **97% at 60 days**

These figures shift the next question upstream—from message content to whether the intervention was actually seen and retained.

---

### What did the statistics help decompose?

The analysis moved from:

**no clear behavior change**

to a more precise hypothesis:

**reach and recognition may be a bottleneck before message content can exert its intended effect.**

It also showed that the apparent association between poster recognition and locking could be explained by pre-existing behavioral tendencies.

That is a core statistical lesson for EBP:

**association is not the same as an intervention effect.**

## How should the findings be interpreted?

### What the study directly shows

The study followed a structured sequence:

1. identify a high-risk context;
2. identify behavioral determinants;
3. design an intervention around those determinants;
4. test it using a cluster RCT; and
5. examine whether the intervention was actually noticed.

Locking rates did not clearly improve, but the evaluation identified a likely implementation bottleneck: insufficient reach and recognition.

### What the study reasonably suggests

A theoretically appropriate message is not sufficient by itself.

For a communication-based intervention to work, it must first be:

- visible;
- noticed;
- read; and
- remembered.

### What the study does not establish

The study does not establish that habit or perceived hassle are irrelevant.

If the intervention was not adequately noticed, the hypothesized behavioral mechanism may not have been fully activated.

The findings therefore illustrate why **theory failure and implementation failure should be distinguished**.

---

## Limitations and the next test

The authors identify limitations including:

- a relatively small control group;
- implementation in settings with relatively low theft risk, where students may have felt less need to lock; and
- posters being placed relatively high, which may have reduced visibility.

The next planned direction was to test more visible tags in higher-risk railway-station settings.

# Research Hub commentary: the bottleneck moved one step upstream

This study is a particularly clear example of a forward-looking use of EBP.

The initial operational problem was:

> too many bicycles are left unlocked.

The research process then decomposed that problem:

```text
Bicycle theft occurs
       ↓
Unlocked bicycles face much greater risk
       ↓
Why do people not lock?
       ↓
Habit and hassle are important
       ↓
Design posters targeting those factors
       ↓
Locking does not clearly change
       ↓
Many target users did not notice or remember the posters
```

Stopping at:

> the poster did not work,

would discard much of the value of the evaluation.

Instead, the evidence suggests that the next bottleneck may be **reach and visibility**, upstream of message content.

The next intervention can therefore shift from only rewriting the message to improving delivery—for example:

- placing prompts closer to eye level;
- attaching visible tags closer to bicycles;
- positioning prompts at the moment of parking or unlocking; and
- testing the approach in higher-risk locations.

These specific adaptations still require evaluation. The key point is that the previous test has made the next hypothesis more precise.

---

## What should practitioners measure?

For posters and other communication interventions, it is useful to distinguish:

1. was the material installed as planned?
2. was it within the target audience’s visual field?
3. did people notice it?
4. did they read it?
5. did they remember the content?
6. did beliefs or attitudes change?
7. did behavior change?
8. did the behavior persist?

This makes it possible to locate the point at which the intervention pathway breaks down.

---

## Related concepts

### Habit

Repeated behaviors may depend heavily on automatic routines rather than deliberate decisions each time.

### Behavioral friction

Small costs such as inconvenience or effort can suppress a behavior that must be repeated frequently.

### Implementation fidelity and reach

An intervention cannot activate its intended mechanism if it does not reach the intended audience.

The low poster-recognition rates in this study make that issue visible.

---

## Key takeaway

**The purpose of evaluation is not simply to declare success or failure.**

This study:

- quantified risk;
- identified behavioral determinants;
- designed an intervention around those determinants;
- tested the intervention;
- found no clear behavioral effect; and
- then identified weak exposure to the intervention as a likely bottleneck.

That sequence is practical EBP:

**measure the problem → decompose its causes → intervene → test → update the bottleneck → design the next intervention.**
